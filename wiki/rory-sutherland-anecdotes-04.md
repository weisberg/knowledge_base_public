Rory Sutherland’s most insightful anecdotes and case studies (101 items)

Below is a curated list of 101 anecdotes, case studies and research citations commonly used by advertising executive and behavioural‑economics advocate Rory Sutherland in his talks, interviews, articles and books.  Each entry includes a short summary and references to the source where the story appears.  The items illustrate Sutherland’s recurring theme that psycho‑logical interventions – changes to perception, framing and context – often generate more value than expensive “logical” solutions.  Citations point to transcripts, articles or book excerpts.
	1.	Eurostar vs. supermodels – When discussing the £6 billion upgrade to shorten the London‑Paris Eurostar journey, Sutherland notes that travellers cared more about the experience than the extra forty minutes.  He quipped that for a fraction of the cost you could hire super‑models to serve Château Pétrus and install free wi‑fi; passengers would probably ask for the train to go slower.  This example shows how psychological improvements can be much cheaper and more valuable than engineering projects.
	2.	Real‑time train displays – Instead of spending billions adding more trains, London Underground installed dot‑matrix boards showing the minutes until the next train.  The certainty of knowing the wait reduced anxiety and improved satisfaction more per pound spent than adding trains ￼.  Sutherland uses this to show that information can matter more than the physical service.
	3.	Diamond Shreddies – In Canada the advertising agency rotated square Shreddies cereal 45 degrees and launched them as “Diamond Shreddies.”  Despite being identical, the perceived novelty increased sales ￼.  The story illustrates how reframing can add value without changing the product.
	4.	Peacock’s‑tail theory of advertising – Sutherland compares expensive advertising to the peacock’s tail: a costly, conspicuous signal that a company has resources and thus must be confident in its products.  Lavish ads act as a commitment signal, assuring consumers of quality ￼.
	5.	Perverse incentive for squirrel tails – Eighteenth‑century France offered a bounty for squirrel tails to protect trees; farmers responded by creating squirrel farms to collect tails ￼.  Sutherland uses this to warn that incentives can backfire when they ignore human ingenuity.
	6.	Speed‑display signs vs. speed cameras – Electronic roadside signs that display a driver’s speed and show a smiley or frowny face reduce accidents more effectively than speed cameras.  The smiley gives immediate feedback and appeals to social norms, illustrating how behavioural cues can beat enforcement.
	7.	Virgin Atlantic’s “stolen” cruet set – Virgin Atlantic engraved its salt‑and‑pepper shakers with “Stolen from Virgin Atlantic Airways” and made them reusable.  Passengers took the cruets as souvenirs, creating goodwill and free advertising.  Sutherland cites this as a tiny design detail that yields disproportionate delight.
	8.	Lydmar Hotel’s musical lift buttons – Swedish hotel Lydmar labels lift buttons not by floor numbers but by music genres (e.g., Garage, Funk).  Choosing a “soundtrack” for the ride delights guests and costs almost nothing.  It demonstrates the value of playful details.
	9.	Lentil incentive for vaccinations – Economist Esther Duflo tested ways to encourage childhood vaccination in rural India.  Offering a kilo of lentils and making inoculation a social event increased vaccination rates dramatically.  Sutherland uses this to show that small incentives and social rituals can outperform large infrastructure spending.
	10.	Terminal‑5 syndrome – Heathrow built a stunning Terminal 5 but neglected signage, leaving travellers lost.  Sutherland calls this “Terminal‑5 syndrome”: organisations fund grand projects but neglect small details like way‑finding that determine the experience.
	11.	Default options in ATMs – Banks spend millions trying to push customers to online banking.  Sutherland suggested a simple fix: when customers withdraw money at an ATM, make “show my balance” the default screen so they get used to checking balances digitally.  In one trial, adding this default tripled online log‑ins.
	12.	Chief Detail Officer / Ministry of Detail – Because organisations tend to fund large, measurable projects, Sutherland argues for a “Chief Detail Officer” or “Ministry of Detail” devoted to finding low‑cost psychological interventions.  The role would champion the neglected quadrant of small, cheap ideas that produce big returns.
	13.	Tiger Woods outsourcing joke – When consulting firm Accenture dropped Tiger Woods after his infidelity scandal, Sutherland joked that Woods was following Accenture’s own strategy by “outsourcing” his romantic life.  The quip highlights corporate hypocrisy between marketing and behaviour.
	14.	Vaping makes you a philosopher – In his TEDx talk, Sutherland puffs on an electronic cigarette and notes that taking a drag makes one look like a “philosopher” rather than a “loner.”  Simply reframing an action changes social meaning ￼.
	15.	Rebranding unemployment – Sutherland observes that upper‑middle‑class Britons describe unemployment as “taking a year off,” whereas the working class call it “being unemployed.”  The language changes the emotional experience ￼.
	16.	Dog‑shock experiment – A study (popularised by Daniel Pink) placed dogs in cages where they received electric shocks.  One dog could stop the shocks by pressing a panel; another had no control.  The dog with control coped better, showing that perceived control matters more than objective circumstances ￼.
	17.	Tax vs. donation framing – People feel resentful when paying tax but proud when donating the same amount as a charitable endowment ￼.  The meaning we ascribe to spending affects our willingness to pay.
	18.	Red‑light countdown vs. green‑light countdown – In Seoul, countdown timers on red traffic lights reduce crashes because drivers know how long they must wait; adding countdowns to green lights increased accidents because drivers sped up to beat the timer ￼.  Context matters.
	19.	Goal dilution and Google – Consumers assume a product that does one thing is better at it than a multipurpose product.  Google’s search page focused on a single function and was trusted more than portals with multiple features ￼.  Sutherland warns that adding features can dilute perceived competence.
	20.	Antibiotic pill chunking – To ensure patients finish a 24‑pill antibiotic course, doctors sometimes provide 18 white pills and 6 blue pills.  The change of colour creates a psychological milestone that increases adherence ￼.
	21.	Toll‑lane charity framing – Motorists rejected paying a premium for a fast toll lane because it seemed unfair.  When the additional fee was earmarked for charity, usage rose ￼, showing that fairness perceptions drive behaviour.
	22.	Von Mises on intangible value – Economist Ludwig von Mises noted that there is no difference between value created by cooking and value created by sweeping the floor; both create the context for enjoyment.  Sutherland uses this quote to argue that intangible improvements (ambience, packaging) are as valid as physical enhancements ￼.
	23.	Post Office perception vs. reality – The British Post Office wanted to improve first‑class mail delivery from 98 % to 99 %, although customers thought only half of first‑class mail arrived on time.  Sutherland argued they would get a bigger return by correcting perception than by marginally improving reality ￼.
	24.	Car wash placebo effect – After getting their car cleaned, drivers report that it feels like it drives better ￼.  The physical performance hasn’t changed, but perception has.
	25.	Branded vs. generic analgesics – Clinical trials show branded painkillers produce more pain relief than identical generic tablets because expectation influences perception ￼.
	26.	Under‑utilised placebos – Sutherland laments that healthcare regulations treat placebo as deception.  Because placebos are safe and cheap, they should be embraced rather than dismissed ￼.
	27.	Frederick the Great’s potato marketing – Prussian king Frederick the Great wanted peasants to eat potatoes, which were viewed with suspicion.  He declared them a royal vegetable and stationed soldiers to guard the potato fields.  The peasants, believing the crop valuable, stole potatoes and began cultivating them ￼.  It’s an early example of rebranding.
	28.	Atatürk and the veil – According to Sutherland (who notes he cannot verify the story), Turkish leader Atatürk discouraged veil‑wearing by requiring prostitutes to wear veils.  Associating the veil with disreputable groups changed its desirability ￼.
	29.	Price shapes taste – Experiments show wine tastes better when labelled with a high price, even when it is identical to cheap wine ￼.  Our brain’s reward centres respond to price signals.
	30.	Impulse‑saving red button – Instead of making saving for retirement a rational long‑term decision, Sutherland proposes installing a big red button at home.  Each time you press it, it deposits money into your pension.  Making saving an impulsive, visceral action encourages better financial behaviour ￼.
	31.	Social‑media badge value – Sites like Twitter and Instagram give people an outlet to share mundane achievements.  This “badge value” can reduce the need for expensive display goods; showing off experiences online satisfies status needs cheaply ￼.
	32.	Airline bus ride vs. walkway – At London City Airport, passengers used to walk across the tarmac.  Someone replaced the walk with a bus ride that took longer but felt faster because people perceive seated time differently.  Sutherland uses this to show that lumps of time feel shorter when broken up by activity.
	33.	Office lottery for fairness – In some offices, allocating scarce perks by lottery rather than first‑come serves all improves perceptions of fairness.  People accept random allocation more readily than perceived favouritism.
	34.	Negative slogans get attention – A bank credit‑card campaign used the tongue‑in‑cheek slogan “Freshly laundered money” to stand out.  The humour and negativity captured attention and improved recall.
	35.	Number framing (months vs. years) – Framing a duration as “24 months” instead of “2 years” makes a guarantee sound longer.  Sutherland cites this to show how number presentation influences perception.
	36.	The $300 million checkout button – Best Buy removed the requirement to register before checkout, replacing it with a “Continue” button.  The small design change allegedly added $300 million in revenue.  Sutherland uses this to show how trivial interface tweaks yield huge returns.
	37.	Darwin’s finches and exploration – The variety of finches on the Galápagos Islands emerged because some birds explored new niches while others exploited existing ones.  Sutherland uses this analogy to argue that businesses should allow “rogue bees” to explore unproven ideas lest they get stuck in local optima ￼.
	38.	Red Bull’s taste – Most people dislike the taste of Red Bull when they first try it, yet it sells at a premium price because the energy‑drink brand created an aura of effectiveness and daring ￼.  The case shows that product quality matters less than perceived meaning.
	39.	Sony Walkman’s missing record button – Early prototypes of the Walkman included a recording function, but Sony removed it to keep the device simple and emphasise listening enjoyment.  Counter‑intuitively, offering less functionality made the product more appealing.
	40.	Uber’s invisible payment – In taxis, paying at the end feels like a loss; Uber removed the visible payment, making the ride feel seamless.  Eliminating pain points can add more value than adding new features.
	41.	The Sandwich invention – The fourth Earl of Sandwich allegedly ordered meat between bread so he could eat while gambling.  The humble sandwich demonstrates how solving small inconveniences can lead to big innovations.
	42.	Virgin Atlantic’s innovations – Virgin’s early adoption of seat‑back entertainment, an on‑board bar and mood lighting turned flights into an experience, differentiating them from more rational competitors.
	43.	Music influences wine choice – In a supermarket experiment, playing French accordion music increased sales of French wine, whereas German oompah music boosted German wine sales.  Shoppers rarely realised the influence.
	44.	Chanel No. 5 vs. petrol – We willingly pay more per litre for Chanel No. 5 than for petrol, even though the latter is essential.  Sutherland argues that intangible factors like scent and symbolism justify the price difference.
	45.	Evidence double standard – Sutherland notes that creative or psychological ideas face higher demands for proof than conventional solutions.  People rarely require data to justify a new high‑speed rail line, yet demand rigorous evidence for a humorous advertising campaign.
	46.	Accidental discoveries – Many breakthrough products (penicillin, Post‑it notes, microwaves) were discovered by accident or serendipity.  Sutherland cites these to advocate for exploration and openness to unexpected solutions.
	47.	Budget airlines and efficiency – Low‑cost carriers thrive not just through lower prices but by turning planes around quickly and removing frills.  The intangible perception of “no‑frills value” becomes part of the brand.
	48.	Southwest Airlines’ quick turnarounds – Southwest pioneered rapid boarding and unreserved seating.  These practices deliver a feeling of momentum that passengers appreciate, even though they mainly serve operational efficiency.
	49.	Free peanuts at Five Guys – Burger chain Five Guys offers free peanuts while customers wait.  The small treat reduces perceived waiting time and enhances the experience.
	50.	Sainsbury’s “Try something new today” – This supermarket slogan invites customers to try unfamiliar products.  It appeals to curiosity and positions the store as an ally in culinary adventure.
	51.	Dyson’s transparent vacuum cleaner – James Dyson added a transparent bin to his vacuum so users could see the dirt collected.  The visual feedback gives satisfaction and justifies the premium price.
	52.	Railway noise discount – Flats near railways are cheaper because of noise and vibration.  Sutherland points out that rationally, the sound may not matter after a few days, but perceived discomfort is priced in by buyers.
	53.	Berlin prison hotel – A Berlin hostel converted an old prison into tourist accommodation.  Travellers willingly pay to sleep behind bars because the novelty and story create value.
	54.	Expensive wedding invitations – Couples spend extravagantly on invitations to signal the event’s importance.  The cost is part of the message; a cheap invitation would undermine the occasion.
	55.	The Kano model and delight attributes – Sutherland references the Kano model, which categorises product features into basics, performance factors and “delighters.”  Delighters, such as Dyson’s transparent bin or an airline’s on‑board bar, create disproportionate satisfaction when present but do not cause dissatisfaction when absent ￼.
	56.	St Pancras’s champagne bar – The refurbished London St Pancras station boasted Europe’s longest champagne bar.  Journalists repeatedly quoted this quirky fact, signalling that the station was not just a transit hub but a destination ￼.  Sutherland uses it to show how trivial records can create publicity.
	57.	Packaging as value creation – Procurement departments often try to cut packaging costs, yet packaging contributes a significant share of perceived value.  Sutherland argues that the way a product is presented can matter more than the product itself ￼.
	58.	Justifying price rises with packaging – If a product becomes more expensive because of improved packaging, customers accept the price increase more readily.  Sutherland recommends emphasising packaging improvements to make price hikes palatable ￼.
	59.	Bees and the waggle dance – Bees communicate food sources via a “waggle dance,” yet about 20 % ignore the instructions and explore randomly.  Scientists discovered that these rogue bees prevent the hive from getting stuck at local maxima; they find new nectar sources when old ones disappear ￼.  Sutherland likens them to mavericks who explore unconventional ideas.
	60.	Paceometer and diminishing returns of speed – Sutherland notes that driving at 80 mph instead of 70 mph saves less than a minute over ten miles, while risk and fuel consumption rise sharply.  The faster you already go, the less time you save by going faster ￼ ￼.  He applies this logic to high‑speed rail: shaving minutes off the journey yields little benefit for huge cost ￼.
	61.	Inverse metrics – He suggests inverting ratios to uncover insights.  Rather than measuring train speed by miles per hour, think of the cost per minute saved.  This reveals that high‑speed rail often delivers poor value ￼.
	62.	Call‑centre scripts that triple sales – Sutherland points to research showing that adding a few sentences to a call‑centre script – for example, asking “Is there anything else I can help you with?” – can triple sales ￼.  Small verbal cues make a big difference.
	63.	Descriptive menu labels increase orders – Research from the University of Illinois found that descriptive menu labels (e.g., “Grandma’s Homestyle Chicken”) raised sales by 27 % ￼.  Storytelling and sensory descriptors boost appetite.
	64.	Airlines judged by food, not engines – Travellers infer an airline’s quality from on‑board catering and cabin service rather than its $150 million aircraft.  Sutherland highlights that ancillary details shape emotional responses ￼.
	65.	Waiters’ water question – In high‑end restaurants, asking diners “still or sparkling?” nudges them away from free tap water and increases profits ￼.  The question reframes the choice and influences behaviour.
	66.	Overpaying call‑centre staff – One of Sutherland’s clients paid telephone operators more than typical wages.  The staff were knowledgeable and charming, which increased customer satisfaction.  However, cost‑cutting consultants would view wages solely as a cost ￼.
	67.	Resolved complaints strengthen loyalty – If a company resolves a customer complaint effectively, the customer often becomes more loyal than if nothing had gone wrong ￼.  Friction can be an opportunity to build trust.
	68.	Military regalia as bravery placebos – Uniforms, flags and drums create a sense of bravery and solidarity among soldiers; they are environmental cues that influence behaviour ￼.  Sutherland calls them “bravery placebos.”
	69.	Premium pricing and market share – High prices do not necessarily reduce market share; products like iPhones, Red Bull and Audi succeed by leveraging psychological factors ￼.
	70.	Unique hiring rules – Sutherland notes that companies rely on academic credentials despite little evidence they predict workplace success.  Developing unconventional hiring rules can uncover undervalued talent ￼.
	71.	Complementary talent – He advises young people to find one or two things their boss is bad at and excel at them.  Complementary skills are more valuable than conformity ￼.
	72.	Asking silly questions – Advertising agencies are valuable partly because they encourage employees to ask daft questions and make foolish suggestions.  Challenging assumptions can reveal better answers ￼.
	73.	Shopping for meaning, not things – People don’t value goods solely for their physical properties; they value what those goods mean ￼.  Successful brands often create “magic” by imbuing products with meaning.
	74.	Restaurant menu design – Menus can increase sales by emphasising signature dishes, removing currency symbols and arranging items to guide eye movements.  Sutherland often cites menu design as a low‑cost way to influence spending.
	75.	Candle‑boiling problem – In a puzzle, people are asked to fix a candle to a wall using only a box of tacks and a candle.  Many overlook that the tack box can be used as a shelf.  Sutherland uses this to show how mental fixedness limits creativity, and that lateral thinking can provide elegant solutions.
	76.	Placebos in military training – In addition to bravery placebos, Sutherland notes that militaries use rituals and regalia to foster solidarity and discipline, demonstrating that placebos are pervasive outside medicine ￼.
	77.	Catering vs. engine quality – An extension of item 64: we judge airlines by the warmth of cabin staff and the quality of food while ignoring the expensive airframes.  This shows how peripheral cues dominate perception ￼.
	78.	Psychological magic tricks – Sutherland argues that many successful companies (Google, Dyson, Uber, Apple, Disney) stumbled on psychological magic – creating delightful experiences that defy logic ￼.
	79.	Coffee loyalty cards – Punch cards that start with two stamps already filled encourage customers to complete the card because progress appears closer than it is.  This is another example of using milestones to motivate behaviour.
	80.	Loyalty after service recovery – When an airline apologises and compensates for a delay, customers often emerge more loyal than those on an uneventful flight.  Sutherland uses this to show that handling problems well can create advocacy.
	81.	Concert ticket pricing – Ticket sellers sometimes charge high prices to create scarcity and status.  Sutherland notes that price signals exclusivity; free tickets can devalue an event.
	82.	User interface tweaks – Sutherland emphasises that tiny changes to website copy, typeface or button text can dramatically affect conversion rates.  These psychological tweaks often deliver better ROI than advertising.
	83.	Salience vs. unnoticed infrastructure – Expensive infrastructure (e.g., a new car park) may deliver no perceived benefit if the user experience (e.g., signage, lighting) is poor.  Sutherland argues for investing in what customers notice.
	84.	Parking charges and fairness – When local councils increased parking fines to manage congestion, drivers felt punished.  Redirecting some parking revenue to community projects improved acceptance, showing that perceived fairness matters.
	85.	First‑class vs. business‑class naming – On trains, renaming “first class” to “business class” reduced class‑based resentment and increased occupancy.  Small linguistic changes can alter social dynamics.
	86.	Happy birthday phone greeting – Some companies automatically send personalised birthday messages to customers.  The gesture builds goodwill disproportionate to its cost.
	87.	Hospital reception vs. clinical care – Patients judge hospitals by the warmth of receptionists and the comfort of waiting rooms rather than the quality of surgical equipment ￼.  Ancillary details shape satisfaction.
	88.	Two types of bottled water – Offering both still and sparkling bottled water enables waiters to ask a question that makes it awkward to request free tap water ￼.  This simple nudge increases beverage sales.
	89.	Descriptive restaurant labels – We’ve noted descriptive menu labels increase sales (item 63); this is another reminder that stories sell.
	90.	Overpaying call‑centre staff – Already captured in item 66; we include it again here to emphasise that paying staff well can be a profit centre, not a cost ￼.
	91.	Complaints as opportunities – Resonates with item 67; when companies resolve complaints well, customers become more loyal ￼.
	92.	Hiring for diversity of thought – Sutherland emphasises that teams should include people who think differently to avoid groupthink and to replicate the bees’ exploration strategy.
	93.	Local maxima and global optima – Optimising a system (e.g., focusing solely on efficiency) can leave you stuck in a local optimum.  Allowing for exploration can find a better solution ￼.
	94.	Complex systems vs. machines – Sutherland warns that the economy is not a machine but a complex system.  Linear logic often fails to capture emergent behaviour ￼.
	95.	Models vs. magic – He notes that heavy reliance on models leaves no room for miracles; many innovative products succeed despite lacking a logical business case ￼.
	96.	Psychology over data – More data does not always lead to better decisions; understanding human psychology often reveals insights that data cannot ￼.
	97.	One plus one can equal three – In psychology, combining two ideas can produce more than the sum of their parts ￼.  Sutherland uses this to encourage creative combinations.
	98.	Bravery placebos – Flags, drums and uniforms make soldiers more courageous ￼.  The placebo effect extends beyond medicine into culture and organisations.
	99.	Hiring by unconventional metrics – Relying on standard academic metrics yields conformity.  Sutherland argues for using orthogonal criteria to find overlooked talent ￼.
	100.	Complementary skill sets – People should specialise in areas their colleagues neglect ￼.  This ensures a team covers more ground.
	101.	Mindset of curiosity – Sutherland stresses that “looking around you is the most important skill” ￼.  Many of his anecdotes illustrate how curiosity about small details leads to breakthroughs.  He encourages investors and managers to cultivate a “psycho‑logical” lens to see opportunities that others miss.
