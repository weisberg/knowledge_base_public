Rory Sutherland frequently touches upon the challenges and unique aspects of selling consulting services, particularly those in the realm of marketing and behavioral science, often contrasting them with more traditional, deterministic business approaches.

Here are some of his anecdotes and insights related to selling consulting services:

*   **The Unpredictable Value of Human Insight vs. Management Consultancy:** Sutherland notes that management consultancy often involves "spending an enormous amount of money on loads and loads of people coming around doing something which will ultimately end up with some fairly boring incremental improvements and people fucking love it" [1]. In contrast, a "human insight" can be gained in an afternoon and make "millions of pounds without needing 400 Oxbridge graduates" [1]. The core problem, he suggests, is that **human insight is often unpredictable**, making it less popular in business settings where predictable, incremental improvements are heavily rewarded, even if less impactful [1]. If something eccentric succeeds, you get a pat on the back, but if something interesting fails, it's "career suicide," creating a fundamental incentive for people to do boring things [1].

*   **Behavioral Science Practice and Website Redesign:** Sutherland recounts an early success of Ogilvy's Behavioral Science Practice. They advised a client that something was "bad about your design of your website" [2]. For an investment of £25,000 to redesign the choice architecture of their website, the client made **£10 million in incremental higher margin revenue** that year and every subsequent year [2]. Despite this clear, substantial return, the client "never came back" [2]. Sutherland speculates that one reason for this counterintuitive response might be that organizations prefer incremental improvements that keep jobs safe longer, rather than large, sudden successes that might make future goals harder to achieve [2]. This highlights the **discomfort with unpredictable, high-impact results** [1, 2].

*   **The Amazon "Call Me Back" Button and the Burden of Proof:** Sutherland describes Amazon's "Call me back" button for customer service as "absolutely brilliant" [3]. He asked another large organization why they hadn't implemented it, and their response was, "We couldn't make the business case work" [3]. He argues that Amazon, being a "fast feedback business," can quickly see if customer experience investments pay off [3]. However, in "slow feedback businesses" like banking, it might take nine years to prove the return on investment for an improved customer experience [3]. This excessive "absurd burden of proof" imposed by finance people prevents these businesses from copying effective, but difficult-to-quantify, customer service innovations, leading to "astoundingly bad" customer service [3, 4]. He emphasizes that **demanding precise attribution and an absurd burden of proof for every single piece of activity stifles potentially valuable initiatives** [5].

*   **The Value of "Anecdotal Information" in Consulting:** Sutherland champions the rehabilitation of the word "anecdote," arguing that most significant information first emerges in anecdotal form [2, 6-8]. He compares this to how police investigations begin: "Do you notice anything unusual last night?" [4, 8, 9]. This open-ended, anecdotal phase is crucial for discovery, even if the information lacks "evidential value" for immediate action or legal proof [4, 8, 9]. He applies this to marketing, stating that the current business world's demand to "only start with things that have evidential value" before acting is a mistake that stifles interesting developments [4]. Entrepreneurs are fascinated by anecdotes, seeing them as opportunities, whereas bureaucrats get angry because it "messes with my internal pre-existing model of the world" [2, 10].

*   **Marketing as a "Casino with Pretty Good Odds":** Sutherland describes marketing as "a fucking casino... but it's a casino with pretty good odds" [5, 11-14]. He argues that a lot of what marketing does is "probabilistic," not deterministic, meaning outcomes aren't perfectly predictable or attributable to a single cause [5, 12-14]. This contrasts with the "deterministic mindset" often found in finance, which seeks everything to be "predictable and within measurable bounds" [5]. He believes that demanding "absurd burden of proof" for every marketing activity is nonsensical and that it's often sufficient to ask if doing more of it in the aggregate leads to a better outcome [5].

*   **The "Scout Bee" Analogy for Innovation and Marketing:** Drawing from evolutionary biology, Sutherland explains that while most "worker bees" efficiently exploit known nectar sources, a smaller percentage of "scout bees" randomly explore for new ones [15, 16]. This exploration, though seemingly inefficient in the short term, is crucial because without these "random bees," the hive would "get trapped in a local maximum and it starves to death" as it fails to adapt to new circumstances [15-18]. He argues that businesses are too focused on exploitation (optimizing efficiency based on past data) at the expense of exploration (experimenting and trying new things) [16]. Marketing and innovation, he suggests, should be judged differently from operational efficiency; they are about **opportunity maximization**, not just operational efficiency [19].

*   **The Need for "Interestingly Less Wrong" Solutions:** In marketing, Sutherland asserts, the goal is not to be "right" in a rigid, deterministic sense, but to be "interestingly less wrong" [20]. This means exploring ideas that are unusual, attract attention, or intrigue people, even if they don't fit perfectly within a conventional logical framework [20]. This approach allows for breakthroughs that purely rational, "right" answers might miss.

Sutherland's anecdotes highlight that selling consulting services that embrace "psycho-logic" and "alchemy" requires challenging deeply ingrained organizational mindsets. It's about convincing clients to invest in ideas that might not initially "make sense" to a spreadsheet or a rigid logical framework, but which hold the key to disproportionately powerful outcomes by leveraging the inherent irrationality and emotional drivers of human behavior. It's like a seasoned detective, not just a forensic scientist, whose gut feeling about an "unusual" detail might lead to solving a case, even if that initial hunch isn't backed by "data" at the outset [9].
