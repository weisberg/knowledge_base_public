# **From Good to Great: A 2025 Retrospective on Enduring Principles and Hard Realities**

## **Section 1: The Premise of Greatness: Beyond the Enemy of Good**

### **1.1 Deconstructing the Core Thesis: Why "Good is the Enemy of Great"**

The central, counterintuitive argument of Jim Collins's *Good to Great* is that the primary impediment to achieving exceptional, sustained performance is not failure, but rather the pervasive condition of being merely "good".1 This thesis posits that for most organizations, the journey to mediocrity is not a product of incompetence but of complacency. When a company achieves a satisfactory level of success—consistent profits, a stable market position, a respectable brand—it often loses the impetus for the arduous and disciplined process required to make the leap to greatness.2 Good, therefore, becomes the enemy of great because it is comfortable, acceptable, and far easier to maintain than the relentless pursuit of excellence.1

The intellectual genesis of this research question was a direct challenge to Collins's previous work, *Built to Last*. A business leader remarked that while insightful, *Built to Last* was of limited utility because it focused on companies that were seemingly "born great," possessing visionary founders and inherent advantages from their inception.4 The more pressing and universal question was whether a company with a long history of average, or merely good, performance could consciously choose a different path and transform itself into an enduringly great organization.4

*Good to Great* was conceived to answer this question: is greatness a matter of circumstance, or is it a matter of conscious choice and discipline?

### **1.2 The Research Mandate: A Rigorous, Empirical Approach**

To answer this question, Collins and his 21-person research team embarked on a five-year study in 1996, designed to empirically identify the factors that distinguish companies that made the leap to greatness from those that did not.5 The methodology was defined by its rigor and a multi-stage filtering process.

First, the team established a demanding definition of "great" performance. Starting with a pool of 1,435 companies that appeared on the Fortune 500 list between 1965 and 1995, they searched for a specific pattern of results: a period of 15 years where cumulative stock returns were at or below the general stock market, followed by a distinct transition point, after which the company generated cumulative returns at least three times the market over the next 15 years.5 This stringent criterion was designed to filter out one-hit wonders and companies whose success could be attributed to a lucky break or a favorable industry wave; the 15-year period of sustained outperformance was considered too long to be a fluke.7 This process yielded a final, elite set of just 11 companies.

The cornerstone of the research, however, was the "matched pair" or "comparison" method.4 For each of the 11 "good-to-great" companies, the team selected a direct comparison company. These control-group firms were in the same industry, had similar opportunities and resources, and often had even better performance than the good-to-great company prior to the transition point, yet failed to achieve sustained greatness.4 This comparative analysis was critical, as it allowed the researchers to isolate the variables that were unique to the good-to-great companies. The final concepts included in the framework were those that appeared as a significant change variable in 100% of the good-to-great companies during their transition years, but in less than 30% of the comparison companies.5

### **1.3 The Foundational Framework: A Sequential Path to Breakthrough**

The research culminated in a conceptual framework that maps a sequential and cumulative process for achieving greatness. This framework is not a checklist of independent variables but an integrated model that unfolds across three broad stages: **Disciplined People**, **Disciplined Thought**, and **Disciplined Action**.1

The sequence is paramount. The findings suggest that an organization must first get the right people in place before it can engage in the kind of honest, disciplined thinking required for true clarity. Only with that clarity can it then take the disciplined, consistent actions that build unstoppable momentum. Disciplined action is impossible to sustain without disciplined thought, and disciplined thought is of little value without disciplined people to act upon it.13

This sequential structure also serves as a powerful diagnostic tool for organizational decline. When a company falters, the symptoms often appear in the realm of "action"—a failed product launch, a poor acquisition, an ineffective restructuring. However, these are rarely the root cause. Tracing the failure backward reveals that a breakdown in disciplined action often stems from a lack of disciplined thought, such as the absence of a clear, guiding strategic concept. This, in turn, is almost always preceded by a failure in the discipline of people—a lack of the right leadership to confront reality or a failure to get the right individuals on the team in the first place. The framework, therefore, provides a logic for both building greatness and understanding its demise.

## **Section 2: Stage I - Disciplined People: The Bedrock of Transformation**

The entire *Good to Great* framework is built upon a foundational stage focused on people. The research concluded that the journey to greatness does not begin with vision, strategy, technology, or restructuring. It begins with "who."

### **2.1 Level 5 Leadership: The Paradox of Humility and Will**

One of the most profound and unexpected discoveries of the study was the type of leadership required to catalyze a good-to-great transformation. In every single case, the transition was helmed by what the researchers termed a "Level 5 Leader." This concept describes a leader who embodies a paradoxical mixture of profound personal humility and intense, unwavering professional will.4 This finding was not an ideological preference but an empirical one, emerging directly from the data.16

The defining characteristics of Level 5 leaders are distinct and often counterintuitive:

- **Ambition for the Institution:** Their primary ambition is for the greatness of the company and its purpose, not for personal wealth, fame, or adulation. They are driven by a cause beyond themselves.11 A key indicator of this is their fanatical drive to set up their successors for even greater success, ensuring the company's greatness endures long after they are gone.4
- **The Window and the Mirror:** When things go well, Level 5 leaders look out the window to apportion credit to other people, external factors, or simple good luck. When results are poor, they look in the mirror, taking full responsibility and never blaming bad luck or others.11
- **Understated and Resolute:** They are often described as quiet, shy, reserved, and self-effacing. They motivate not through a larger-than-life, charismatic personality but through inspired standards and a stoic determination to do whatever it takes to make the company great.16

This leadership style stands in stark contrast to the high-profile, celebrity Level 4 leader, often a "genius with a thousand helpers," who can produce impressive results but whose ego-driven approach frequently leads to a decline in the company's fortunes after their departure.4 While the question of whether Level 5 leadership can be taught remains open, Collins suggests that many people have the latent potential—the "seed"—which can be cultivated through conscious personal development, self-reflection, mentorship, or formative life experiences.16

### **2.2 First Who... Then What: Assembling the Bus**

The second key concept within the "Disciplined People" stage is the principle of "First Who... Then What." The good-to-great leaders understood that their first task was not to figure out where to drive the bus, but to get the right people on the bus, get the wrong people off the bus, and get the right people into the right seats.8 Only then did they turn their attention to the "what"—the strategy, the vision, the direction.

This people-first approach is grounded in three simple truths:

1. **Adaptability in a Changing World:** If people join a company primarily because of its direction, what happens when that direction needs to change? The organization faces a significant problem. But if people are on the bus because of who else is on it, a change in direction is far easier to execute.3 In a chaotic and unpredictable world, a busload of adaptable, high-performing people is the ultimate strategy.22
2. **The End of Motivation Problems:** With the right people on board, the problem of how to motivate and manage them largely disappears. The right people are self-motivated by an intrinsic drive to produce the best results and be part of creating something great. They do not need to be tightly managed or "fired up".4
3. **The Primacy of People over Vision:** A great vision without great people to execute it is, in the end, irrelevant. Conversely, great people will eventually figure out how to create a great vision.4

Practically, this principle manifests in a culture that is "rigorous, not ruthless".1 Rigor means consistently applying exacting standards at all levels, especially in people decisions. It involves three key disciplines:

- **When in doubt, don't hire—keep looking.** The cost of filling a seat with the wrong person is far greater than the cost of leaving it empty for a while longer.23
- **When you know you need to make a people change, act.** The moment a leader feels the need to tightly manage someone, a hiring mistake has been made. Delaying the decision is unfair to all the right people on the bus who must compensate for the underperformer's inadequacies.4
- **Put your best people on your biggest opportunities, not your biggest problems.** Managing problems can only make you good, whereas building opportunities is what can make you great.7

This "First Who" principle also explains the study's surprising finding that executive compensation was not a key factor in the transition to greatness. The research found no systematic link between specific compensation structures and performance. The logic is simple: it's not *how* you compensate your executives, but *which* executives you have in the first place. The right people will do the right things regardless of the incentive system, because their internal moral code demands it. The purpose of compensation, therefore, is not to get the right behaviors from the wrong people, but to attract and retain the right people to begin with.18

Furthermore, the "First Who" principle serves as the most effective antidote to organizational bureaucracy. Bureaucracy, defined as a system of rules designed to compensate for incompetence and a lack of discipline, becomes largely unnecessary when an organization is filled with self-disciplined people.13 The wrong people necessitate rules and micromanagement. These very rules, in turn, frustrate and drive away the right people, who thrive on freedom and responsibility.8 This creates a downward spiral where more wrong people lead to more bureaucracy, which repels more right people.13 By focusing on "First Who," an organization breaks this cycle at its source, creating the conditions for a non-bureaucratic, high-performance culture to emerge.

## **Section 3: Stage II - Disciplined Thought: The Clarity of Focus**

Once the right people are on the bus, the journey moves to the stage of Disciplined Thought. This phase is about confronting reality with unvarnished honesty and then, from that place of clarity, developing a simple yet profound frame of reference to guide all future decisions.

### **3.1 Confronting the Brutal Facts: The Stockdale Paradox in Practice**

The research showed that every good-to-great company began its path to greatness by confronting the brutal facts of its current reality.4 The principle holds that a series of good decisions is impossible without this initial, unflinching assessment. This requires a specific psychological duality, which Collins termed the

**Stockdale Paradox**.

Named for Admiral Jim Stockdale, a prisoner of war in Vietnam for eight years, the paradox is defined as follows: *You must maintain unwavering faith that you can and will prevail in the end, regardless of the difficulties, AND at the same time, have the discipline to confront the most brutal facts of your current reality, whatever they might be*.24

Stockdale observed that the prisoners who did not survive were the optimists. They would set unrealistic deadlines for their release—"we'll be out by Christmas"—and when those dates came and went, they would die of a broken heart.27 The paradox, therefore, is a rejection of blind optimism in favor of a grounded hope. It is the ability to hold two seemingly contradictory thoughts simultaneously: the faith in an ultimate victory and the acceptance of a harsh present reality.

Creating a climate where the truth is heard and brutal facts can be confronted is a primary task of leadership. The study identified four basic practices to achieve this:

1. **Lead with questions, not answers.** Good-to-great leaders used a Socratic method, asking questions not to corner or manipulate, but to genuinely gain a deeper understanding of reality.4
2. **Engage in dialogue and debate, not coercion.** All good-to-great companies had a culture of intense, often loud, debate. The purpose was not to give people a "say" in a predetermined decision, but to engage in a collective search for the best answers.4
3. **Conduct autopsies, without blame.** When mistakes are made, the focus should be on learning and understanding, not on assigning blame. A culture without blame allows people to bring forth brutal facts without fear of reprisal.4
4. **Build "red flag" mechanisms.** These are systems that turn information into information that cannot be ignored. The key is not in having better information—the comparison companies had access to the same data—but in creating processes that force attention to critical facts.4

### **3.2 The Hedgehog Concept: Simplicity Within Three Circles**

After confronting the brutal facts, the next step in disciplined thought is to develop a simple, crystalline concept to guide the organization's efforts. This is the **Hedgehog Concept**, derived from an ancient Greek parable that states, "The fox knows many things, but the hedgehog knows one big thing".5 The fox, with its cunning and complex strategies, is consistently defeated by the hedgehog's simple, masterfully executed defense of curling into a spiky ball. For companies, this translates to the power of focused simplicity over scattered complexity.

The Hedgehog Concept is not a goal or a strategy but a deep *understanding* that exists at the intersection of three circles 5:

1. **What you can be the** **best in the world** **at.** This is the most critical of the three circles. It requires a brutally honest assessment of what the organization has the potential to do better than any other company on the planet. It is not about a core competence; a company might have a competence in something it can never be the world's best at. Conversely, what it can be the best at might be something it isn't even doing yet. This circle demands an egoless clarity about both strengths and, just as importantly, weaknesses.31
2. **What drives your economic engine.** This involves discovering the single economic denominator—such as profit per employee, profit per customer, or profit per store—that has the most significant and sustainable impact on the company's economic health. Identifying this single metric provides profound insight into how to generate robust cash flow and profitability over the long term.8 For social sector organizations, this circle is adapted to a "resource engine," focusing on the key drivers of sustained resources, including cash flow, volunteers, and emotional commitment.32
3. **What you are deeply passionate about.** Great companies focus on activities that ignite their passion. The key is not to try and stimulate passion for a profitable venture, but to discover what the people in the organization are genuinely passionate about and build the enterprise around that.8

Achieving this level of clarity is an iterative process, not a single event. It emerges from years of confronting the brutal facts and making a series of good decisions that build upon one another.15 The most crucial distinction is that a Hedgehog Concept is an

*understanding* of what you *can be* the best at, not a goal or an intention *to be* the best.15

Operationally, the Hedgehog Concept functions as a powerful strategic filter. Its primary role is not to generate a to-do list, but to provide a simple, rigorous mechanism for deciding what *not* to do. Fanatical adherence to the concept means systematically shunning opportunities—even those that seem great or once-in-a-lifetime—if they fall outside the intersection of the three circles.8 While comparison companies often fell into an "undisciplined pursuit of more," chasing fads and making reactive acquisitions, the good-to-great companies used their Hedgehog Concept as a tool of disciplined refusal. This protected their most valuable resource—focus—and ensured that all organizational energy was channeled in a single, consistent direction, thereby setting the stage for the Flywheel effect.

## **Section 4: Stage III - Disciplined Action: The Physics of Momentum**

With disciplined people engaged in disciplined thought, the final stage is to translate that clarity into disciplined action. This is where the potential for greatness is converted into reality through a culture of execution, the strategic application of tools, and the relentless accumulation of momentum.

### **4.1 A Culture of Discipline: Freedom Within a Framework**

A great company cannot be built on discipline alone; many highly disciplined organizations have marched with precision straight into disaster. The *Good to Great* framework requires a specific kind of culture: one that combines a culture of discipline with an ethic of entrepreneurship.13

This is not a culture of tyranny or top-down control. A tyrannical leader can produce short-term results, but the discipline is external and unsustainable; once the leader departs, the organization often falters.25 The good-to-great culture is built from the ground up, starting with self-disciplined people. It is based on the idea of "freedom and responsibility within a framework".7 The organization hires self-disciplined individuals who do not need to be managed and gives them the freedom to deliver on their responsibilities. The "framework" is the clear and simple Hedgehog Concept.

In this environment, people operate with extreme diligence and intensity, but they do so by choice. They are not just cogs in a machine; they are responsible owners of their outcomes. This culture obviates the need for the stifling bureaucracy found in most organizations. The logic is sequential and powerful: when you have disciplined people, you don't need hierarchy. When you have disciplined thought, you don't need bureaucracy. When you have disciplined action, you don't need excessive controls.3 This magical alchemy of freedom and responsibility allows for superior performance to be sustained over the long term.

### **4.2 Technology Accelerators: The Strategic Application of Innovation**

In a world often mesmerized by technological disruption, one of the study's most salient findings was the role of technology in the good-to-great transitions. The research concluded that technology, in and of itself, is never a primary cause of greatness or decline. Rather, technology is an **accelerator of momentum, not a creator of it**.8

The good-to-great companies did not begin their transformations by pioneering new technologies.26 Their approach was thoughtful and strategic. They first achieved clarity on their Hedgehog Concept and began building momentum. Only then did they pioneer the application of carefully selected technologies to accelerate that momentum. If a company has a clear sense of direction, technology can help it get there faster. If a company is headed in the wrong direction, technology will only help it get there sooner.35

This strategic application was often characterized by a "Crawl, Walk, Run" approach.8 Instead of being early adopters driven by a fear of being left behind, these companies would first experiment with a new technology on a small scale (crawl), then figure out how it could be best applied to their existing model and Hedgehog Concept (walk), and only then would they make a major commitment to implement it at scale (run).8 The case of Walgreens and its methodical adoption of internet technology to enhance its core convenience concept is a prime example, standing in stark contrast to the reckless "run first" approach of many failed dot-coms.35

### **4.3 The Flywheel and the Doom Loop: The Inexorable Path to Breakthrough**

The culmination of the entire *Good to Great* process is captured in the powerful metaphor of the **Flywheel**. The transformation from good to great is not a single, dramatic event or a "miracle moment." From the outside, it may look like a revolutionary breakthrough, but from the inside, it feels like a slow, organic, and evolutionary process.38

The process is akin to pushing a giant, 5,000-pound flywheel. The first push barely moves it. With persistent, concerted effort, it completes a single, slow rotation. More pushes, all in a consistent direction, and it begins to turn a little faster. Each turn builds on the work done before, compounding the effort. Eventually, a point of breakthrough is reached. The momentum of the massive wheel kicks in, and it begins to spin faster and faster, its own weight now working in its favor, creating almost unstoppable momentum.8

There is no single "big push" that creates the breakthrough; it is the cumulative effect of all the pushes. The Flywheel connects all the other concepts into a cohesive whole. Level 5 leaders provide the humble discipline to keep pushing. "First Who" gets the right team to push together. "Confronting the Brutal Facts" and the "Hedgehog Concept" ensure all the pushes are applied in a consistent and correct direction. The "Culture of Discipline" makes consistent pushing an intrinsic part of the organization's DNA. "Technology Accelerators" act like grease on the axle, reducing friction and speeding up the flywheel's rotation.

In stark contrast is the **Doom Loop**, the pattern exhibited by the comparison companies. Lacking the patience for the slow buildup phase, they would look for a "miracle" program or a revolutionary leader to jump-start the process.38 They would push the flywheel in one direction, and when immediate, dramatic results failed to materialize, they would stop, change course, and lurch in a new direction. This was often accompanied by reactive, ill-conceived mergers and acquisitions designed to create momentum, which almost always failed.38 This constant lurching back and forth, driven by reaction rather than disciplined thought, ensured that they never built sustained momentum and remained stuck in a cycle of disappointing results and frantic, inconsistent action.2

## **Section 5: A 2025 Retrospective: Enduring Wisdom and Critical Updates**

Published in 2001, *Good to Great* became one of the most influential business books of its generation. Now, 24 years later, the business landscape has been radically transformed by globalization, financial crises, digital disruption, and the rise of artificial intelligence. This new context demands a critical re-examination of the book's findings. How have the 11 "great" companies fared? How have the book's core principles held up against the test of time and the scrutiny of critics? And how must these concepts be adapted to remain relevant for leaders in 2025 and beyond?

#### **Case Studies in Durability and Decline**

The divergent paths of these companies provide powerful lessons.

**The Failures: Circuit City and Fannie Mae**

**Circuit City** stands as a near-perfect case study in the inversion of the *Good to Great* principles. Its decline from 2001 to its 2009 bankruptcy was marked by a series of decisions that directly violated the book's core findings. Instead of Level 5 humility, its leadership exhibited arrogance, failing to react to the competitive threat of Best Buy.43 It abandoned its Hedgehog Concept by exiting the profitable appliance business in 2000, a move that confused customers and ceded ground to competitors.43 Most fatally, it violated the "First Who" principle in the most dramatic way possible: in 2007, it fired 3,400 of its most experienced, highest-paid salespeople to cut costs, gutting its primary competitive advantage in customer service and triggering a collapse in morale and performance.43

**Fannie Mae**'s story is more complex, highlighting the limits of internal discipline in the face of systemic market failure and government intervention. While the company was lauded for its performance, its business model was deeply intertwined with the U.S. housing market. The subprime mortgage crisis of 2008 exposed its immense risk, leading to a government takeover to prevent a catastrophic collapse of the financial system.45 Fannie Mae's failure demonstrates that even a company with disciplined internal practices can be overwhelmed when its entire ecosystem is built on a flawed and unstable foundation.

**The Scandal: Wells Fargo**

The **Wells Fargo** account fraud scandal, which came to light in 2016, presents the most profound challenge to the book's findings. The discovery that employees had created millions of fraudulent accounts to meet aggressive sales quotas suggests that what was perceived as a "Culture of Discipline" may have been a toxic, high-pressure sales culture that incentivized unethical behavior on a massive scale.67 This scandal directly questions the nature of the company's leadership and culture during its "great" years. It suggests a catastrophic failure of Level 5 leadership, where executives prioritized short-term metrics and personal bonuses over the long-term health and integrity of the institution, leading to immense reputational damage that persists to this day.71

**The Acquired: Gillette**

**Gillette**'s story is one of successful integration rather than independent greatness. Its acquisition by Procter & Gamble in 2005 was seen as a "dream deal" that combined two consumer goods powerhouses.73 The Gillette brand has remained a dominant force, but its post-2005 performance is a reflection of P&G's strategy. The brand has also faced significant disruption from direct-to-consumer (DTC) upstarts like Dollar Shave Club, which eroded its once-dominant market share by challenging its high-price, high-tech model.48 Gillette's response, including its controversial "The Best Men Can Be" ad campaign, reflects a broader strategic shift in how legacy brands must now compete on values and cultural relevance, not just product innovation.77

**The Enduring and Evolving: Nucor and Abbott Laboratories**

**Nucor** and **Abbott Laboratories** stand out as the clearest examples of sustained high performance. **Nucor** has continued to thrive by doubling down on its core principles. Its Hedgehog Concept—being the lowest-cost, most efficient, and most innovative steel producer—remains intact. Its "Culture of Discipline" is legendary, built on a foundation of radical decentralization, egalitarianism, and a pay-for-performance system that aligns the interests of every employee with the company's success.80 Nucor has used its culture as its ultimate competitive advantage and has leveraged technology (electric arc furnaces) as a true accelerator, becoming a leader in sustainable steel production.83

**Abbott Laboratories** demonstrates greatness through strategic evolution. Recognizing that its diversified businesses faced different market challenges, Abbott made the bold move in 2013 to spin off its research-based pharmaceutical division into a new company, AbbVie.42 This allowed the legacy Abbott to focus on its strengths in medical devices, diagnostics, and nutrition, while the more volatile but high-growth pharmaceutical business could operate independently. This strategic split, a classic Hedgehog-like decision to focus on what each entity could be best at, has created enormous value for shareholders of both companies.42

### **5.2 Re-evaluating the Research: The Halo Effect, Survivorship Bias, and Other Critiques**

The divergent long-term performance of the *Good to Great* companies lends credence to several significant methodological critiques that have been leveled against the book over the years.

**The Halo Effect:** The most formidable critique comes from Phil Rosenzweig's 2007 book, *The Halo Effect*.86 Rosenzweig argues that much of business research is tainted by a cognitive bias where a company's overall performance (the "halo") shapes our perceptions of its specific attributes like leadership, culture, and strategy. When a company is successful, we retrospectively attribute its success to brilliant leadership and a dynamic culture. When that same company falters, we reinterpret those same attributes as arrogant leadership and a complacent culture.89 This suggests that Collins's research may have committed the fallacy of "connecting the winning dots," inferring causation from correlation. The characteristics identified in the great companies may not have been the

*cause* of their success, but rather a *story* attributed to them because they were successful.86

**Survivorship Bias and Regression to the Mean:** Critics also point to survivorship bias and the statistical certainty of regression to the mean.91 By selecting a small sample of companies based on a period of extraordinary outperformance, the study was statistically likely to have picked firms that were at their absolute peak and were bound to perform less exceptionally in the future.93 The subsequent underperformance or failure of companies like Circuit City, Fannie Mae, and Walgreens supports the argument that their "greatness" was a temporary state, not a sustainable characteristic derived from a magic formula.91

**Context and Luck:** Finally, the framework has been criticized for understating the role of external factors, industry dynamics, and pure luck in organizational success.90 The downfall of Fannie Mae, for example, had more to do with a systemic financial crisis than a failure of its internal discipline at that moment. Collins himself addressed this critique in his subsequent book,

*Great by Choice*, where he introduced the concept of "Return on Luck," acknowledging that both good and bad luck are constants, and greatness depends on what a company does with the luck it gets.

These critiques do not necessarily invalidate the principles themselves, but they do challenge the book's claim to have discovered immutable, scientific laws of business performance. A more nuanced view, as suggested by some academics, is to treat *Good to Great* not as a scientific roadmap, but as a collection of insightful "fables" or a source of inspiration grounded in deep historical analysis.94

### **5.3 Timeless Principles in a Transformed World (August 2025)**

Despite the valid critiques and the mixed performance of the original companies, the core concepts of *Good to Great* retain a remarkable, if adapted, relevance in the business environment of 2025.

**"First Who, Then What" in the Age of AI:** This principle has arguably become *more* critical in an era of rapid technological change. As artificial intelligence and automation make specific technical skills ("what") increasingly transient or obsolete, the ultimate competitive advantage shifts to the innate human capabilities of the team ("who").95 Hiring for character traits like self-discipline, adaptability, curiosity, and a relentless learning mindset is the best strategy for navigating a future you cannot predict.22 An organization filled with the "right people" will be able to learn, adapt, and leverage new tools like AI far more effectively than an organization that simply hires for a specific, and likely fleeting, technical need.

**The Hedgehog Concept in a Disruptive Environment:** The call for unwavering focus seems, at first glance, to be at odds with the modern demand for agility and pivoting. However, the discipline of the Hedgehog Concept is not about rigid stasis. A more modern interpretation might be a "Dynamic Hedgehog." The *process* of seeking the intersection of the three circles remains a vital strategic discipline. But in a disruptive world, the *answers* to the three questions must be continuously re-evaluated. What an organization can be the best at may change as technology evolves. What drives the economic engine may shift with new business models. This continuous re-evaluation, combined with what Collins later termed "Productive Paranoia"—obsessively asking "What if?" and building reserves for unforeseen disruptions—allows a company to maintain its focus while adapting to a chaotic environment.97

**The Flywheel in the Platform Economy:** The Flywheel metaphor has been explicitly and powerfully adopted by the modern tech and startup ecosystem, particularly in the context of platform business models and product-led growth (PLG).100 In these models, the "pushes" on the flywheel are discrete user actions within a product or platform. For example, a user evaluates the product (the first push), which leads them to activate a free trial (the second push), which leads to adoption and regular use (more pushes). As they expand their use and advocate for the product to others, they add more energy and attract new users, creating a self-reinforcing, compounding loop of growth.100 The concept perfectly describes the physics of network effects and viral growth that power today's most successful digital companies.

**A Culture of Discipline in the Modern Workplace:** The concept of a "Culture of Discipline" based on "freedom and responsibility" is more aligned with the modern workplace than ever before. Today's knowledge workers demand autonomy, purpose, and trust.102 A culture that hires self-disciplined people and then gives them the freedom to achieve their objectives is perfectly suited to remote and hybrid work models. It replaces the need for bureaucratic oversight with a culture of accountability and shared values, which is essential for fostering engagement, preventing burnout, and attracting top talent in a competitive labor market.103

Ultimately, the long-term analysis of the *Good to Great* companies reveals a final, powerful paradox. The failure of some of these companies does not invalidate the book's principles; it validates them. The companies that fell from grace did so precisely because they stopped doing the things that made them great. Circuit City abandoned its people-first discipline. Wells Fargo's leadership lost its humility and allowed its culture to become toxic. Greatness, it turns out, is not a destination one arrives at, but a dynamic state of being that requires constant, conscious, and disciplined practice. The principles are not a one-time vaccine against mediocrity, but a continuous regimen that must be followed with unwavering resolve. In that sense, the core message of *Good to Great*—that greatness is a choice—is as relevant and challenging in 2025 as it was in 2001.
