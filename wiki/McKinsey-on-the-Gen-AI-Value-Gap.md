# McKinsey on the Gen AI Value Gap

A synthesis of McKinsey Quarterly's diagnosis of why gen AI adoption surged but enterprise EBIT impact didn't, what distinguishes "AI high performers," and the recommended path to closing the value gap.

---

## The Paradox in One Paragraph

In 2024, nearly eight in ten organizations deployed gen AI across at least one business function — a staggering acceleration from half the year before. Yet according to McKinsey's Global Survey on AI, **more than 80 percent of these companies report no material contribution to earnings** from their gen AI initiatives. Only 1 percent view their gen AI strategy as mature *(MQ 2025 Q4, "Seizing the agentic AI advantage")*. This is the defining paradox: ubiquitous adoption coupled with negligible financial impact. Gen AI is everywhere except on the P&L. The technology that promised $2.6T–$4.4T in additional value has remained a playground for pilots rather than a driver of enterprise transformation. The gap between promise and performance represents both massive capital waste and a strategic vulnerability for organizations that haven't grasped why their AI investments fail to scale.

## The Arc of McKinsey's Thinking, 2024 → 2026

McKinsey's perspective evolved markedly: from a diagnosis of organizational unpreparedness to a prescription for systematic rewiring, and now to the recognition that only agentic AI can close the value gap.

**Q2 2024 — the wake-up call.** *"A generative AI reset: Rewiring to turn potential into value in 2024"* declared that "the generative AI payoff may only come when companies do deeper organizational surgery on their business" *(MQ 2024 Q2)*. Pilots are easy; scaling them to value is hard because it requires broad changes to how work gets done. Companies seeking a shortcut — implementing ChatGPT or GitHub Copilot — would be disappointed. **Rewiring meant overhauling five dimensions:** where to apply gen AI (domain advantage, not commodity use cases); talent (gen AI-specific skills); structure (centralized teams, federated governance); architecture (built for scale, not POC); data (proprietary, accessible, governed).

**Q3 2024 — execution.** The cover package *"Making generative AI work"* shifted tone from prescription to practical execution. Leaders were "far beyond the stage of admiring the technology's potential" and "now focused on how to use it to create value," but the blueprint remained unclear *(MQ 2024 Q3)*.

**Q4 2024 — divergence.** *"The state of AI in charts"* introduced hard data. 65 percent of organizations were regularly using gen AI (nearly double the prior year), but adoption skewed toward horizontal copilots. **Only 5 percent qualified as "AI high performers"** — using gen AI in three+ business functions, more likely to deploy customized or proprietary models, more attentive to risk and data quality *(MQ 2024 Q4, "Lessons from AI high performers")*. Value creation was concentrated, not distributed.

**Q1 2025 — gateway technology.** *"Agents of change: Driving transformation through AI"* reframed gen AI as a gateway technology. **Employees were ahead of organizations** — 21 percent using gen AI heavily (daily), 70 percent lightly — while leaders lagged in capturing organizational value. The prescription shifted to **domain-based transformation**: reinventing entire workflows at the level of specific business domains rather than function by function *(MQ 2025 Q1, Outlook)*.

**Q4 2025 — the agentic answer.** The cover package announced the next inflection. The paradox now had a proposed solution: **AI agents** — autonomous systems combining planning, memory, and integration to transform gen AI from a reactive copilot into a proactive virtual collaborator capable of automating complex business processes *(MQ 2025 Q4)*. Agents are the answer to why horizontal gen AI failed and why only "high performers" willing to deeply reinvent processes succeeded.

## Why the Gap Exists: The Specific Failure Modes

McKinsey identifies compounding failures:

**Horizontal vs. vertical imbalance.** Nearly 70 percent of Fortune 500 companies deployed horizontal solutions like Microsoft 365 Copilot — designed to enhance individual productivity. Gains are real but **"spread thinly across employees"** and not visible in top- or bottom-line results. Vertical use cases (embedded in specific functions) have higher impact potential but **fewer than 10 percent of deployed use cases progress past pilot.** Those that do typically operate reactively *(MQ 2025 Q4)*.

**Pilot purgatory.** Organizations launch countless POCs with "no chance of scaling." The ease of deploying off-the-shelf tools — most projects take 1–4 months — creates a false sense of progress. Customized or proprietary projects take 1.5× longer and face higher friction. Many companies move to the next pilot rather than push through, generating no compounding value *(MQ 2024 Q4, "Take, shape, or make gen AI?")*.

**The Taker trap and the moat problem.** About half of reported gen AI uses rely on off-the-shelf models with little or no customization. As Sukharevsky warns: **"If you have it, your competitor probably has it as well."** The real question is "What is our moat?" The answer is customization — but most organizations aren't investing in it. They're treating gen AI as a commodity to be deployed uniformly when sustainable advantage comes from proprietary models, proprietary data, and specific business context *(MQ 2024 Q4)*.

**Incomplete organizational surgery.** Q2 2024's diagnosis still holds. Launching a few tools is not rewiring. The real changes — restructuring talent, redefining roles and incentives, building centralized governance, establishing data standards, redesigning workflows — are far harder than procurement and training. Most organizations embed gen AI into existing ways of working rather than reimagining workflows from the ground up. *"Companies hoping that gen AI offers a shortcut past the tough — and necessary — organizational surgery are likely to meet with disappointing results"* *(MQ 2024 Q2)*.

**Data and infrastructure debt.** **70 percent of gen AI high performers cite data challenges as their top obstacle**, including ability to integrate data into AI models *(MQ 2024 Q4)*. Most organizations lack the data architecture, metadata standards, and governance to feed proprietary information into models at scale. **Roughly 90 percent of available organizational data is unstructured** (video, images, text) and remains largely inaccessible.

**Talent in the middle.** Organizations have hired senior gen AI engineers, but lack the **middle layer** — people who translate business problems into gen AI applications, understand where gen AI creates competitive vs. commodity value, and manage cultural/process change. The best gen AI talent requires design skills, contextual understanding, collaboration, forensic skills (diagnosing failures), and anticipation skills — far more than coding *(MQ 2024 Q2)*.

**Governance lag.** Only 21 percent of organizations with AI adoption have established policies governing employee gen AI use. Without governance, organizations cannot manage risk, control costs, or align gen AI projects with business strategy. High performers are significantly more likely to have governance and risk-mitigation processes *(MQ 2024 Q2)*.

## The Take/Shape/Make Framework (Q4 2024)

Three archetypes for implementing gen AI, each with distinct strategic implications:

**Takers** use off-the-shelf solutions with little or no customization (enterprise copilots, public chatbots). About half of reported gen AI uses fall here. Appropriate for productivity in non-differentiating functions (e.g., customer service summarization). **Offers no competitive advantage** since competitors have the same tools.

**Shapers** customize off-the-shelf tools with proprietary data and systems. Example: integrating an LLM with a company's CRM, transaction data, and customer histories to generate personalized upsell recommendations. Moderate customization (2–4 months). **The "sweet spot" for most organizations** — balancing speed/cost-efficiency with proprietary differentiation.

**Makers** develop foundation models from scratch. Most expensive and time-consuming; typically only feasible for tech companies, large tech-enabled firms, or enterprises with specific domain requirements (e.g., pharma drug-discovery models).

McKinsey's recommendation: **a well-orchestrated mix of all three.** Sukharevsky: *"The spine and brain of the enterprise of the future will rely on a well-orchestrated mix of multiple foundational models — both off-the-shelf and tools that have been finely tuned to the enterprise's specific need."* Most companies should be Shapers in value-critical domains and Takers for baseline productivity. Maker is reserved for domains where proprietary models are existential.

## What AI High Performers Do Differently

McKinsey surveyed nearly 900 organizations and identified only 46 (5 percent) where "a meaningful share of EBIT can be attributed to their deployment of gen AI." High performers diverge from peers:

**Deployment breadth and depth.** Average of three business functions vs. two for others. Less likely to rely on off-the-shelf options; more likely to use substantially customized or proprietary foundation models *(MQ 2024 Q4)*.

**Risk and governance rigor.** Paradoxically, **high performers are more likely to report negative consequences from gen AI** — not a sign of failure but of maturity. They're further along, pushing gen AI into more critical processes, and have formal processes to identify and mitigate risks. Significantly more likely to embed testing/validation in release processes and to iteratively improve outputs *(MQ 2024 Q4)*.

**Data mastery as prerequisite.** 70 percent cite data challenges (the highest obstacle). This reflects reality — they're actually using gen AI on proprietary, mission-critical data. They've invested in infrastructure, governance, and the work of customizing models with their proprietary data *(MQ 2024 Q4)*.

**Domain reimagination.** They approach gen AI as a catalyst for **reimagining entire domains**, not adding tools to existing workflows. The question moves from "How do we use gen AI?" to "How do we reimagine this business process with gen AI at the core?"

## Rewiring the Organization: The Six Capabilities

From Q2 2024, drawing on McKinsey's *Rewired*:

1. **Define where gen AI creates competitive advantage.** Not all use cases. Identify domains where gen AI delivers distinctive value — unlocking new revenue or driving meaningful cost reductions in mission-critical functions. Maintenance and field service: identifying failures early has direct economic impact. Customer service: a commodity function. **Many organizations bought thousands of GitHub Copilot licenses without a clear value thesis.**

2. **Upskill talent with gen AI-specific skills.** 2–3 months to develop competence; **apprenticeship and community-of-practice models scale fastest.** Critical skills beyond coding: design (where to focus solutions), context (relevant high-quality answers), collaboration (with domain experts), forensic (diagnosing failures), anticipation (edge cases).

3. **Form centralized teams to establish standards.** Develop and enforce protocols enterprise-wide. Procure models, define data readiness standards, set up approved prompt libraries, allocate resources. Operate as a **platform enabling distributed teams**, not a gatekeeper. Squad: data engineers, gen AI-experienced data scientists, plus risk/compliance/legal contributors.

4. **Build technology architecture for scale.** Most organizations underestimate production cost. A basic chatbot takes a week to build, but a stable, accurate, compliant version at scale takes four months — with **model costs <10–15 percent of total solution cost.** Reuse technology (30–50 percent faster development), enable efficient connections between gen AI models and internal systems via standard APIs and reference architectures, prioritize testing/QA over rapid development.

5. **Ensure data quality and leverage unstructured data.** Invest in data architecture targeted at priority use cases. Clarify decision rights, define data processes, establish taxonomies, focus quality efforts on AI applications driving value. Crucially: **understand the value locked in unstructured data** (videos, slides, text, reviews, emails) and establish metadata standards.

6. **Build trust and reusability.** Ensure model accuracy, provide explainability (citations to source documents), test for consistency, train end users on limitations. Design for reusability — establish standards for prompts, context, and components so they can be reused across multiple use cases.

## The Data- and AI-Driven Enterprise of 2030

McKinsey's Q4 2024 vision *(Tavakoli, Harreis, Rowshankish, Bogobowicz)*:

- **Data everywhere.** Employees have latest data at fingertips; data embedded in systems, processes, channels, decision points to drive automated actions (with human oversight). Quantum-sensing for real-time product performance data; gen AI agents informed by historical customer data interacting with digital twins of customers; LLM clusters analyzing health data for personalized medicine.
- **Capability pathways over disconnected use cases.** Cluster technology components into reusable pathways serving multiple use cases. An automotive company built two pathways — customer segmentation/analysis (PySpark, Databricks, ML libraries) and personalized communication (LLMs, sales DW, customer-360) — that served personalized offers, service ops prompts, and sales staff intelligence.
- **Mastery of unstructured data.** Unlock the 90 percent of enterprise data currently inaccessible. Heavy investment in NLP, tagging, cleansing, privacy protection, bias testing, continuous recalibration.
- **Leadership shift.** The chief data and analytics officer evolves from risk-focused to **business-focused innovator** responsible for making data easy to use, track, and trust. **50 percent of current CDOs feel unable to drive innovation using data; 70 percent of even high-performing companies report difficulties** in data governance and AI integration.

## The Agentic Answer

Q4 2025 reframes the paradox through agents. **An agent is not a tool that responds when prompted; it is an autonomous system with planning, memory, integration, and ability to take independent action toward defined goals.** Traditional gen AI tools operate reactively — answering when asked. Agents operate proactively — monitoring conditions, anticipating needs, executing tasks without human prompt.

This explains why horizontal copilot implementations generated minimal EBIT impact: they improve individual task efficiency without fundamentally changing how processes operate. An agent can automate an end-to-end workflow — processing customer data, checking inventory, confirming pricing, updating systems, following up — autonomously. **That's the level of impact required to move from "no material EBIT contribution" to meaningful business transformation.**

## Practical Playbook for Closing the Value Gap

1. **Conduct a domain analysis.** Map critical business domains — not functions, but end-to-end workflows or journeys (customer onboarding, product development, supply chain fulfillment). For each, identify mission-critical processes, abundant proprietary data, and significant potential business impact.

2. **Define your gen AI strategy clearly.** For each domain decide: Taker (off-the-shelf for commodity functions), Shaper (customized with proprietary data for differentiated value), or Maker (proprietary models for specialized use cases). Most organizations: Shaper for value-critical domains, Taker for baseline productivity.

3. **Establish centralized governance and standards.** Central gen AI office responsible for approving high-impact projects, data standards, approved model libraries, prompt repositories, vendor relationships, risk/compliance protocols. Enabler, not bottleneck.

4. **Build or acquire the right talent.** Hire 2–3 senior engineers/data scientists who have built gen AI products at scale to lead. Simultaneously launch targeted upskilling for existing data teams. Communities of practice scale knowledge.

5. **Invest in data architecture and governance.** Identify which proprietary data sources are most valuable for priority use cases. Metadata standards, quality processes, governance rules. Plan for unstructured data integration.

6. **Design for scale from day one.** Allocate **85–90 percent of effort** to deployment, testing, integration, compliance. Reusable components, standard APIs, approved tools. Test rigorously. Build for iterative improvement, not one-time perfection. Measure on EBIT contribution, not project completion.

7. **Transform organization around AI, not AI around organization.** Largest barrier is organizational, not technical. Change roles and incentives (align teams around domains, not functions). New operating rhythms (agile funding, outcome-focused governance). Shift leadership mindset from "How do I implement this tool?" to "How do I reimagine this workflow with AI at the core?"

8. **Rigorously track impact to EBIT.** Most pilots fail not because tech doesn't work but because organizations can't articulate the path from pilot to EBIT. Define financial benefit upfront for each project. Measure relentlessly. **If a project isn't progressing toward material business impact within six months, stop it.**

9. **Plan for continuous improvement.** Gen AI governance must evolve as technology matures and you scale. Embed testing, validation, iterative improvement in release processes. Monthly or quarterly reviews of model performance, data quality, risk posture.

10. **Prepare for the agentic inflection.** Significant value creation will increasingly depend on agentic AI — systems that autonomously execute workflows. Begin designing workflows with agents in mind even if your current implementation is copilots. **Organizations that reimagine end-to-end processes for autonomous execution will capture disproportionate value in the next phase.**
