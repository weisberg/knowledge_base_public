Definition of Competitive Intelligence

Competitive   intelligence   (CI)   is   the   systematic   process   of   gathering   and   analyzing   information   about

competitors and the broader market environment to inform strategic decisions

1

. Unlike basic market

research, CI aims to turn external data into actionable insights that help a company gain a competitive edge

2

. This function is distinct from other business areas – it is not the same as sales (which drives revenue),

finance (which manages internal financials), or marketing (which promotes products). Instead, CI focuses

outward:   it   monitors   rival   products,   pricing,   customer   sentiment,   and   industry   trends,   then   feeds   this

intelligence  into  various  departments.  Many  large  organizations  have  even  formalized  CI  as  a  separate

team or role. For example, bigger companies often dedicate full-time staff to CI, whereas smaller firms

might assign CI duties to product marketing or general management

3

. In practice, CI intersects with

multiple functions –  sales teams  use CI to refine deal tactics,  marketing  uses it to adjust positioning,

strategy  teams use it to map competitive moves – but the CI function itself centers on external insights

rather   than   internal   operations.   As   Investopedia   notes,  “within   any   organization,   competitive   intelligence

means different things to different people and departments”

4

, yet its core mission remains constant: arming

decision-makers   with   knowledge   about   the   competitive   landscape   to   avoid   surprises   and   seize

opportunities

5

6

.

CI in E-Commerce for Premium vs. Mass-Market CPG Brands

Competitive   intelligence   has   become   vital   for   consumer   packaged   goods   (CPG)   brands   selling   physical

products on Amazon. Both premium brands and mass-market brands deploy CI, but their use cases and

focus areas often differ based on their market positioning.

Premium Brands: Tactics and Case Study

Trends and Tactics: Premium CPG brands (e.g. high-end or luxury-oriented products) use CI to protect their

brand equity and respond to price-focused competition on Amazon. A key concern is the rise of lower-cost

“copycat” products that mimic premium offerings

7

. These copycats often undercut on price, threatening

the premium brand’s value proposition. Premium brands also monitor Amazon advertising closely – for

instance, they track competitors bidding on their branded keywords, which can drive up the cost of ads and

siphon away customers

7

. Additionally, premium players pay attention to competitor sales velocity and

reviews; a fast-selling rival product with strong ratings can erode the natural advantages of a premium

brand over time if not countered. In each of these cases, premium brands rely on smarter operations and

analysis   to   “claw   back”   their   advantages

7

.   This   might   include   optimizing   fulfillment   (to   maintain   fast

Prime shipping), highlighting quality differentiators in content, or adjusting pricing strategies carefully (e.g.

bundling products or offering limited coupons to stay competitive without eroding a premium image).

Mass-Market Brands: Tactics and Case Study

Trends and Tactics: Mass-market CPG brands – typically large manufacturers of everyday consumer goods

– use competitive intelligence at scale to drive market share on Amazon. Their focus is often on the “digital

shelf” and real-time market dynamics. This includes tracking competitors’ pricing daily (and using dynamic

pricing   when   possible),   monitoring   promotions   and   coupon   activities,   and   ensuring   their   own   product

visibility remains high. A recent industry analysis noted that success on Amazon now requires a  “complete

view of your sales and competitors… in your categories”

10

 – mass-market brands achieve this by aggregating

huge   amounts   of   Amazon   data.   They   commonly   track   metrics   like   share   of   search   results,   category

rankings, and stock availability for all major rivals. For example, if a competitor’s product goes out-of-stock

or loses the Buy Box, a mass-market brand wants to know immediately to capitalize on the opportunity

(perhaps by boosting its own ad spend or ensuring its inventory can meet demand).  Speed  is a theme:

decisions are made in days or hours, not weeks, so CI for mass-market players emphasizes real-time or at

least   daily   updates.   Another   tactical   use   case   is  assortment   and   innovation  –   large   brands   analyze

competitor product portfolios and new launches on Amazon to identify gaps. If data shows a rival gaining

traction with a new flavor, size, or format, the brand can quickly develop a response (or even use Amazon’s

market data to guide its R&D). Mass-market companies also use CI to benchmark content and reviews: they

often   compare   product   page   content,   image   quality,   and   average   star   ratings   against   top   competitors,

aiming   to   outperform   on   these   drivers   of   conversion.   In   short,   CI   for   mass-market   CPGs   is   about

operational   excellence   at   scale  –   ensuring   no   competitor   move   goes   unnoticed   and   that   their   own

execution online is best-in-class.

Tools Used by Premium vs. Mass-Market Brands

The competitive intelligence tools employed often differ between premium niche brands and mass-market

CPG   giants,   reflecting   differences   in   budget,   scale,   and   objectives.   Below   is   a   comparison   of   how   each

typically approaches CI toolsets:

•

Scope and Data Collection:  Premium brands (especially smaller companies or emerging brands)

tend to use readily available Amazon seller tools or agency services to gather intelligence. Their data

scope might be narrower – focusing on a specific category or a handful of key competitors – and

sometimes involves manual research or affordable software. For example, a premium startup brand

might use a tool like Helium 10 or Jungle Scout to  track competitor keywords, prices, and sales

estimates on Amazon, gaining insights without a huge investment. These tools allow them to spy on

how   rivals   are   performing   and   identify   niche   opportunities.   Mass-market   CPG   companies,   by

contrast,   typically   ingest  large-scale   data  from   multiple   sources.   They   deploy   enterprise-grade

analytics platforms that automatically scrape thousands of product listings, track real-time pricing

across retailers, and integrate with internal systems. Their CI data collection is continuous and far-

reaching – a mass-market manufacturer might monitor an entire category’s top 100 products every

day for stock levels, pricing changes, search rank, and more. The emphasis is on automation and

comprehensive coverage of the competitive landscape.

•

Goals and KPIs:  Premium brands often use CI tools with the goal of defending their unique value

and finding pockets of growth. Their analysis might zero in on branding and customer perception

metrics  –   for   instance,   monitoring   reviews   closely   for   quality   issues   or   watching   if   a   budget

competitor’s lower price is actually hurting that competitor’s reviews (indicating customers feel “you

get what you pay for”). They also use CI to guide storytelling and differentiation. A premium organic

food brand might track competitor ingredients and certifications on Amazon so they can highlight

their superior attributes. Mass-market brands, on the other hand, align CI tools to volume-driven

KPIs. Market share, sales ranking, and price competitiveness are paramount. The tools they use

produce dashboards showing market share percentages, price indices, and promotional frequency.

Their goal is often to be the #1 seller in their category on Amazon, so the CI function measures

progress toward that – e.g. daily share-of-voice in search results, percentage of top keywords where

their   product   is   ranked   above   competitors,   etc.   In   summary,   premium   brand   CI   is   more   about

qualitative  differentiation   (are   we   maintaining   our   premium   image   and   hitting   the   right   niche

demand?) while mass-market CI is about quantitative dominance (are we outselling and outspending

competitors across the board?).

3

•

Tool Types and Analysis Methods: There is a notable divergence in the tools themselves. Premium

brands  frequently   leverage   all-in-one   Amazon   seller   suites   that   provide   competitive   insights

alongside other functions. Helium 10, for example, is popular among smaller brand owners for its

broad toolkit – it offers over 30 different tools covering product research, keyword tracking, listing

optimization, and competitor analytics

15

. A premium brand can use such a suite to identify which

search terms competitors rank for, track when a rival changes their product title or images, or even

get alerts on price drops. These tools often present analysis in a user-friendly way (graphs of sales

rank over time, keyword overlap with competitors, etc.), which suits lean teams that may not have

data analysts on staff. Mass-market companies often invest in dedicated enterprise CI platforms

or   build   custom   data   solutions.   They   might   use   providers   like   Profitero,   Stackline,   or   Edge   by

Ascential,   which   feed   raw   data   and   insights   into   the   company’s   analytics   pipeline.   The   analysis

methods here are more complex: data scientists or CI analysts at the company will slice the data to

find patterns (for example, correlating a competitor’s price promotion with a drop in the company’s

own sales, to quantify the impact). These tools support deeper dives – e.g. a mass-market brand can

analyze how a competitor’s new product launch affected category search trends week-by-week. The

outputs are often detailed reports or dashboards for various teams. It’s common for mass-market CI
tools   to   integrate   with   internal   BI   systems   so   that   marketing,   sales,   and   supply   chain   teams   all

consume   the   competitor   data   relevant   to   them.   In   effect,   mass-market   brands   treat   CI   data   as

another big data stream in their operations.

•

Budget and Accessibility: As expected, premium niche brands usually have smaller budgets for CI

tools, whereas mass-market firms allocate substantial resources. Premium brands gravitate toward

cost-effective solutions – many of the seller tools (Helium 10, Jungle Scout, etc.) offer subscription

plans ranging roughly from $50 to a few hundred dollars per month

16

17

. These tools can be

incredibly powerful for the price, giving smaller brands access to sales estimates and keyword data

that  level  the  playing  field  with  bigger  competitors.  Mass-market  brands,  however,  often  opt  for

enterprise   subscriptions   that   can   cost   thousands   per   month.   For   instance,   Stackline   –   an   e-

commerce intelligence platform often used by large brands – is known for its robust features (like

estimating competitors’ online sales and managing ad campaigns)  but comes with a high price

tag, starting around $5,000+ per month

18

. Such an investment is justified for mass-market firms

because the scale of potential gains (or losses) on Amazon is huge – a slight market share shift in a

billion-dollar   category   can   mean   millions   in   revenue.   Additionally,   enterprise   tools   typically   allow

unlimited   users   and   advanced   support,   fitting   the   needs   of   large   cross-functional   teams.   In

summary, premium brands focus on affordable, nimble tools that still provide rich Amazon data,

while  mass-market   brands   invest   in   large-scale,   integrated   platforms  to   drive   competitive

analysis at volume.

Impact of Artificial Intelligence and Agentic AI on CI

Artificial intelligence (AI) – particularly the rise of agentic AI (autonomous AI agents) – is rapidly reshaping

the competitive intelligence function. Recent advancements in AI are enabling CI teams to do more in less

time,   and   even   to   foresee   competitive   moves   before   they   happen.   Here,   we   explore   the   current   and

forecasted impact of AI on CI over the next 2–5 years, along with implications for how companies organize

around CI and strategy.

Automation   and   Real-Time   Analysis:  AI   is   dramatically   accelerating   data   collection   and   analysis   in

competitive   intelligence.   Machine   learning   algorithms   can   process   vast   amounts   of   online   data

continuously, far beyond human capacity. In fact, “artificial intelligence and machine learning are transforming

competitive intelligence by automating data collection and analysis processes,” allowing patterns and insights to

be uncovered that would be too time-consuming for human analysts

34

. In practical terms, AI-driven tools

can   monitor   competitor   product   listings,   prices,   reviews,   news   mentions,   and   even   social   media   24/7

without   fatigue.   This   leads   to   a   shift   from   periodic,   manual   CI   reports   to  real-time   intelligence

dashboards. Modern CI platforms are increasingly integrating AI agents that constantly scan for changes –

for example, an AI agent can instantly flag that a competitor has run out of stock of a top-selling item or

that a new competitor product has appeared in the category. Forbes recently noted that businesses can

“stop being blindsided by competitor moves” by building real-time CI dashboards, as  “AI agents excel at

monitoring   and   analyzing   competitive   information”  and   can   alert   companies   to   strategic   developments

immediately (e.g. a sudden price cut by a rival)

35

. This real-time, always-on nature of AI is making CI

proactive rather than reactive. Companies that adopt AI in CI can respond to threats or opportunities in

hours instead of weeks, a critical advantage in e-commerce where prices and rankings change daily.

AI Agents as “Digital Analysts”:  The concept of  agentic AI  refers to AI systems that can autonomously

plan and execute tasks. In the context of CI, agentic AI means having AI “teammates” that handle significant

portions of intelligence work with minimal human direction. Competitive intelligence AI agents are already

emerging. These agents “work tirelessly to monitor competitors, predict trends, and provide actionable insights”,

essentially   acting   as   always-on   digital   analysts   for   the   business

36

.   For   example,   a   CI   agent   might   be

tasked with tracking all competitor product launches in a category. The agent will not only gather that data

but   also   analyze   it   –   perhaps   identifying   that   Competitor   X’s   new   product   is   climbing   the   sales   rank

unusually fast, and then predicting the trend that this product could overtake the company’s product in 2

weeks if nothing is done. The agent could then alert the CI manager or even recommend a response (such

as   running   a   counter-promotion   or   increasing   advertising).   AI’s   ability   to   ingest   diverse   data   (pricing,

advertising, customer reviews, search trends) and find correlations greatly enhances forecasting. It can flag

7

weak signals that a human might miss – for instance, an AI might detect that a rival’s product has received a

surge   of   positive   reviews   after   a   formula   change,   predicting   a   likely   boost   in   their   sales   ahead.   This

predictive aspect of AI will make CI not just about reporting the past, but about anticipating the future.

Over the next 2–5 years, expect CI tools to increasingly incorporate predictive analytics modules, where AI

models forecast competitor behavior (e.g. “Competitor Y is likely to expand into Category Z next quarter

based on their hiring and patent activity”) and market shifts. This will enable companies to strategize with

foresight, creating contingency plans before changes hit.

all accessible via natural language queries or automated alerts. The implication for organizational structure

is   profound:   CI   knowledge   silos   break   down   when   AI   can   deliver   tailored   insights   to   each   department

instantaneously. Each team (marketing, product, sales, etc.) can have AI-curated intelligence feeds relevant

to   their   functions.   This   democratization   means   companies   might   not   need   large   CI   teams   to   manually

liaison with every department; instead, a smaller CI team could focus on high-level analysis and strategy

while the AI agents disseminate routine intel across the org.

Shifts   in   CI   Roles   and   Skills:  As   AI   takes   over   data   crunching   and   monitoring,   the   role   of   human   CI

professionals is evolving. Rather than spending hours on collecting data, CI analysts and managers will

focus more on interpretation, validation, and strategic advising. AI is a powerful tool, but it still requires

human   judgment   to   ensure   insights   are   accurate,   contextual,   and   ethically   gathered.   One   emerging

responsibility is training and tuning AI systems – CI teams might work on refining an AI agent’s parameters

(e.g. telling it which competitors or signals are most important, or feeding it proprietary data to combine

with public info). Another key skill is sense-making: humans will verify AI findings and connect the dots with

business strategy. For instance, if an AI agent flags an upcoming competitor move, the CI manager will

translate what that means for their company’s product roadmap and communicate that to leadership. We’re

likely to see CI roles shift from “information hunters” to “strategic storytellers” – leveraging AI-generated

intelligence to craft strategic recommendations. Some companies are already embedding CI within strategy

or product teams, given that AI can handle much of the basic analysis. Organizationally, CI might become

less of a standalone department and more of a hybrid function overlapping with strategy, data analytics,

and marketing. At large firms, we may see a role like “CI Technology Lead” or “AI-enabled CI Analyst” whose

job is to manage the suite of AI tools and ensure the rest of the business is extracting value from them.

8

AI Adoption and Competitive Parity:  It’s worth noting that adoption of AI in competitive intelligence is

quickly becoming the norm. In a 2024 survey of 700+ CI professionals,  25% were already using AI tools

and 56% planned to adopt AI in the near future

39

. Moreover, nearly half of CI teams said they now use

AI on a daily basis in their workflow

39

. The most common uses today include summarizing competitor

content (cited by 67% of CI teams using AI) and even directly helping in data collection of intel (50%)

40

.

These figures show that AI isn’t a hypothetical future – it’s here, and competitors might be using it. This

raises the stakes for everyone: if all players have AI scouting the field, the competitive tempo increases.

Companies will need to innovate not just in whether they use AI, but how they use it. Those who integrate

AI most effectively – combining AI’s speed with human creativity – will likely pull ahead. In the next 2–5

years, failing to leverage AI in CI could put a company at a serious disadvantage, akin to “flying blind” while

others have augmented vision. On the flip side, over-reliance on AI without human oversight could lead to

mistakes (like acting on incorrect data or AI hallucinations), so the strategic challenge will be balancing

efficiency with accuracy.

Strategic   Implications:  The   infusion   of   AI   into   competitive   intelligence   ultimately   makes   organizations

more agile and more  intelligent  in their strategic moves. With agentic AI handling real-time competitive
monitoring, companies can adopt a strategy of  continuous adaptation. For example, pricing strategy on

Amazon   can   become   highly   dynamic   –   AI   might   adjust   prices   or   promotional   spend   in   response   to

competitor actions in near-real time (some advanced sellers already use algorithmic repricers; future AI will

be   even   more   nuanced,   perhaps   factoring   in   competitor   inventory   levels   or   sentiment   analysis   from

reviews). From an organizational strategy perspective, planning cycles may speed up. Instead of annual

competitor   reviews   and   static   strategic   plans,   companies   might   move   to   rolling   strategy   development

where AI-fed insights prompt strategy tweaks monthly or weekly. This could blur the line between strategy

and execution, as AI can trigger tactical changes that cumulatively shape strategy. Leaders will need to

ensure that short-term AI-driven optimizations align with long-term strategic goals – a new area of focus for

strategic planning. Another implication is resource allocation: if AI highlights that a competitor is investing

heavily in a new channel or product feature, companies might reallocate R&D or marketing budget faster

than before to counter that. Essentially, AI enables a more fluid and responsive strategic posture.

In   conclusion,   artificial   intelligence   –   and   autonomous   AI   agents   in   particular   –   are   transforming

competitive intelligence from a slow, manual practice into a fast, integrated, and predictive function. We can

expect CI tools to evolve into intelligent assistants that provide  real-time, context-aware insights  to all

corners of an organization. Companies that embrace these technologies will likely structure themselves to

be  more  cross-functional  and  data-driven,  with   CI  insights   flowing   through  every  decision   process.  The

enduring “evergreen” principle amid this change is that knowledge is power: that hasn’t changed. What’s

changing   is   how   quickly   and   broadly   that   knowledge   can   be   obtained   and   acted   upon.   In   the   AI   age,

competitive intelligence stands to become not just a department or tool, but an omnipresent capability –

one that could very well be the deciding factor in which brands lead and which lag in the highly competitive

e-commerce arena.
