# McKinsey on AI, Talent, and the Workplace

A synthesis of McKinsey Quarterly's research on the workforce transformation gen AI is causing, what talent strategy must change, the leadership skill set now required, and practical recommendations for HR leaders and CEOs.

---

## Opening: People Before Tech

Gen AI is reshaping work faster than most organizations can manage. McKinsey's central finding across two years of research: companies are treating this as a technology challenge when it is fundamentally a **people challenge**. Gen AI is not a problem for IT to solve in isolation — it's a workforce technology that touches **88 percent of workers in non-technical roles**, from managers and warehouse workers to nurses. This simple fact upends how organizations should think about AI adoption.

The real opportunity (and peril) lies in recognizing that AI's impact on productivity, innovation, and competitive advantage flows through people, not machines. Organizations that adopt a "people before tech" mindset emerge with stronger competitive positioning, better retention, and genuine transformation. Those that lead with technology and force-fit people into new systems struggle with disengagement, top-talent exodus, and failed implementations.

## Who Actually Uses Gen AI: A Surprising Portrait

McKinsey's mid-2023 survey of US, UK, and Canadian employees segmented gen AI users *(MQ 2024 Q3, "People before tech")*:

- **2% — creators.** Help build AI models and develop tools others use. Predominantly software engineers, programmers, ML scientists.
- **8% — heavy users.** Employ AI for most core tasks or to enhance work functions. Designers using AI for 3D modeling, data scientists verifying code semantics.
- **18% — light users.** Use AI for less than 50 percent of primary tasks. Middle managers (meeting notes), educators (classroom activities), comms professionals (drafting), journalists (research).
- **70% — non-users.** Currently unaffected by or unaware of AI's impact. Not permanent: as scope expands, McKinsey expects most to become light or heavy users within a few years.

**88 percent of all gen AI users are in non-technical roles.** Administrators, cashiers, customer care, educators, healthcare workers, HR staff, managers, nurses, retail associates, salespeople, supervisors, warehouse workers. Only 12 percent are technical employees (developers, engineers, IT, programmers); just 2 percent are AI-adjacent technical talent (data analysts/engineers, data scientists, ML specialists).

This distribution shatters the narrative that gen AI is a "tech problem." It's a workforce problem affecting the vast majority of the labor force in roles traditionally thought of as non-technical.

## The Retention Crisis: Talent Exodus Over Poor AI Strategy

Of workers who self-identify as gen AI creators and heavy users, **51 percent plan to leave their jobs within 3–6 months** *(MQ 2024 Q3)*. The general workforce baseline is 34 percent considering departure. **The most AI-capable workers are at significantly elevated flight risk.**

This matters because companies say they want to build gen AI talent in-house. **57 percent of organizations** plan to close gen AI skill gaps via in-house capability building, with upskilling (28 percent) and reskilling (23 percent) as primary tools. Yet these are precisely the workers they are at risk of losing.

The root cause is **not money.** Compensation ranks **seventh** on the list of factors that keep creators and heavy users engaged. Top priorities:

1. Workplace flexibility (40%)
2. Meaningful work (33%)
3. Support for employee health and well-being (31%)
4. Safe workplace environment (29%)
5. Career development and advancement (27%)
6. Reliable and supportive coworkers (27%)
7. Adequate total-compensation package (23%)

Among creators and heavy users who stay, **72 percent report feeling engaged — but 55 percent report clinical levels of burnout**, far exceeding the global average of 32 percent. Companies are extracting productivity from these workers at the cost of their wellbeing.

The implication: **builders and advanced users of gen AI are high-flight-risk populations.** Retaining them requires prioritizing flexibility, meaning, supportive leadership, and community over incremental pay bumps.

## Rethinking the Skills Strategy: From Roles to Skills

McKinsey's Q4 2024 deep-dive *"The gen AI skills revolution"* (Hussin et al.) presents a fundamental rethinking. The old model — defining roles and hiring for them — is breaking down.

### The Problem with Role-Based Thinking

Gen AI is changing the software development life cycle so rapidly that **roles themselves are unstable.** A 2022 developer job description doesn't fit in 2025. Rather than forecast stable roles, organizations should think in terms of **skills as data** — modular, identifiable, continuously mapped against business needs.

McKinsey notes: **70 percent of economic top performers use their own software to differentiate from competitors**, vs. half of peers. Yet most organizations lack the talent pipeline. The challenge is acute: **product manager productivity has improved 40 percent** with gen AI, and coding time has been cut in half — yet only 13 percent of organizations systematically use gen AI in software engineering, and current tools assist only 10–20 percent of typical developer activities.

### New Skills, Newly Urgent

Three critical skill areas for engineers in the gen AI age:

**Review and forensics.** As gen AI generates code, developers shift from "doers" to "reviewers." Effective reviewers evaluate compatibility with existing repositories/architectures, ensure maintainability, spot duplicates and errors, and develop **advanced forensics skills** to identify subtle issues. They must also learn how to train AI tools — giving feedback, identifying which tasks provide the best learning opportunities.

**Connect and integrate.** As organizations combine multiple AI agents, engineers need skills in selecting, combining, and integrating different applications and models. Understanding when one model can quality-control another, how to create feedback loops, how to orchestrate multi-agent workflows.

**Design and upstream skills.** With gen AI handling basic coding, engineers must develop higher-level upstream skills — writing user stories, developing code frameworks/libraries, understanding business outcomes, anticipating user intent. Communication becomes critical; engineers must articulate thinking to non-technical stakeholders.

For product managers, the shift is equally profound. PMs must develop:
- **Gen AI technology use** — low-code/no-code tools, iterative prompting, agentic frameworks where LLMs work together to complete tasks.
- **Adoption and trust building** — empathy to identify barriers (over-trust, under-trust); risk-check integration throughout the product life cycle.

### Skills, Not Roles

McKinsey argues that talent management built on fixed roles and competency matrices is "no match" for gen AI volatility. The new approach:

1. **Treat skills as data.** Use AI/LLMs to infer skills from role descriptions, LinkedIn profiles, code repositories. Build a skills database with clear language, expertise levels, hierarchies. See what you have and what you need.
2. **Plan workforce needs around skills.** Forecast specific skills required by business strategy, not roles. A life sciences company used AI skills inference to scan vacancies, job descriptions, and internal data, building a comprehensive view that fed back into organizational planning.
3. **Continuously reassess.** As new gen AI tools come online, regularly evaluate which skills they replace and what new training is needed.

### Apprenticeship Over Coursework

Standard training is insufficient. McKinsey emphasizes apprenticeship: senior experts coding and reviewing alongside juniors, shadowing, mentoring. **One audio company made apprenticeship explicit: top engineers completed a boot camp on gen AI, then each led a 3–4 day boot camp for 10–15 colleagues, followed by two weekly sessions for three months for ongoing Q&A.**

## The Leadership Angle: AI Muscle for Next-Generation Leaders

Gen AI's impact on leadership is qualitatively different from previous technology shifts.

### Chief AI Officers and Organizational Governance (Q1 2025)

The Q1 2025 *"Agents of change"* outlook notes: most respondents use gen AI at work, and **nearly all employees express optimism** (98% non-users, 91% light users, 80% heavy users). Yet leaders are slow to orchestrate organization-wide adoption.

Three steps:

1. **Establish governance** — centralized structure, often with a **chief AI officer** leading. Accountability, transformation infrastructure, progress monitoring.
2. **Treat it as true transformation** — not a tech initiative but org transformation with clear roles, responsibilities, measurement criteria, regular cadence adjustments. C-suite, board, and BU leader buy-in.
3. **Address employee mindsets and behaviors** — culture change. Help employees see themselves as agents of change rather than passive recipients.

### Building the "Second Muscle": Domain Owner Leadership (Q1 2026)

The real bottleneck is at N-2 / N-3 — domain owners (two to three levels below the CEO) leading end-to-end business transformations *(MQ 2026 Q1, "Building the AI Muscle of Your Business Leaders")*.

**Analysis of Fortune 500 senior leaders found that only 17 percent of their skill set is technical, and only 5 percent of their careers included a technical role.** That's the problem: the most effective domain owners need a **second muscle** — the ability to:

- **Reimagine their domain** with the customer at the center, understanding pain points, using creativity to redesign workflows around AI rather than automating existing processes.
- **Develop AI-enabled transformation roadmaps** — work with domain experts and technologists to map sequenced use cases with KPIs tied to business outcomes — and **not delegate this to IT**.
- **Oversee tech delivery** with enough technical depth to prioritize, solve roadblocks, challenge thinking, and iterate.
- **Lead end-to-end change management** — own adoption and scaling, not just solution development.

How leaders build the second muscle:

**Learn like it's your mission.** Top domain owners spend **6–10 hours/week** on tech learning — reading, conferences, fintech meetings, courses, visits. Hathi (Schwab) took a 30-hour cybersecurity course to deepen knowledge relevant to her board role.

**Fixate on value, not technology.** Start with "What's the biggest problem worth solving?" — not "What can AI do?" Rule of thumb: **opportunities with 20%+ incremental value potential.** Less tech-savvy leaders "say they don't understand tech" and hand problems to IT, resulting in constrained thinking and marginal improvements.

**Understand the operational levers.** Boyd (Citizens Bank): the value equation has three legs — **people, data, and technology.** Van der Winden (Tata Steel) won't commit to a solution without a senior business owner, a sound business case, and good data.

**Get your hands dirty.** No substitute for learning on the job. Hathi participated in design workshops, dug into issues, worked with engineers and data scientists. She could then speak to the board with credibility about investment decisions.

**Develop a nose for tech talent.** Top engineers/architects generate **10× more value** than peers. Successful leaders invest heavily in recruiting, incentive design, learning journeys. Van der Winden trained over 500 engineers by embedding experienced team members with new people on each use case.

**Get comfortable with ambiguity and stage-gate efforts.** Breaking from incrementalism means tolerating ambiguity. Domain leaders achieve some economic benefits with each tranche of investment, constantly pivoting as roadblocks emerge.

### What CEOs Should Do

To build a bench of domain owners with strong AI muscles:

1. **Identify which core business processes have the most AI transformation potential** and assess whether current leaders have the traits above. **Change 20–30 percent of leaders if needed.**
2. **Launch a strategic upskilling program** covering process reengineering, AI models, data management, engineering talent, change management. Combine coursework with hands-on practice — consultants, "two-in-a-box" leadership (tech lead paired with business leader), real capstone projects.
3. **Fix the operating model.** Embed engineering talent in teams led by domain owners. Shift to **persistent funding** (budgets tied to transformation roadmaps, not individual projects). Align performance management and incentives with transformation KPIs.

The upshot: organizations need **75–150 domain owners** (assuming 15–30 core business processes). A bench of tech-capable leaders creates durable competitive advantage — hard to replicate, specific to your business.

## Recommendations for CHROs and Talent Leaders

1. **Stop hiring only for roles; start mapping and forecasting skills.** Build a skills database (use AI inference if possible) treating skills as modular data. Update quarterly.
2. **Redesign retention strategy around flexibility, community, and meaning — not compensation.** For creators and heavy users, flexibility and meaningful work rank far above pay. Audit your EVP and remove compensation from the center of gravity.
3. **Create apprenticeship programs with senior mentorship built into performance expectations and time allocation.** Don't rely solely on formal training. Have your best engineers/architects spend structured time teaching peers.
4. **Establish a mandatory, sequenced AI upskilling program tied to business outcomes.** Executive immersion, technical boot camps, broader learning journeys.
5. **Identify and support your "fearless learners"** — domain leaders driven by curiosity. Time, resources, permission to learn. Rotate promising leaders through tech-depth roles (CDO, software subsidiary, chief digital officer) before they become domain owners.
6. **Address burnout in heavy users and creators.** Health/wellbeing support, reasonable workload, career development. **A 55-percent burnout rate among top AI talent is unsustainable** and signals productivity is being extracted at the cost of retention.
7. **Link HR strategy to the CEO's AI transformation agenda.** HR cannot be a support function here. It is a critical lever. McKinsey: HR plays "an especially important role in gen AI transformations, both by transforming the people domain and by acting as a gen AI copilot for all employees."
8. **Close the gap between what employees want and what employers offer.** McKinsey's 2025 HR Monitor survey found a persistent gap: **41 percent of HR professionals cite total compensation as a top retention driver, while only 28 percent of employees do.** Employees rank relationships, meaning, and job security higher.

## Recommendations for CEOs and Senior Leaders

1. **Establish centralized governance with a chief AI officer or equivalent.** Coordinate adoption, define transformation infrastructure, monitor progress, ensure cross-BU accountability.
2. **Commit to domain-based transformation, not one-off pilots.** 15–30 core business processes or customer journeys with accountable domain owners (N-2/N-3) and clear roadmaps/KPIs.
3. **Build a bench of 75–150 domain owners.** This is the bottleneck. Review your current N-2/N-3 population. Are 17 percent technical in background? If not: recruit, rotate, upskill.
4. **Redesign the operating model for business-led AI delivery.** Embed engineering and data talent in cross-functional teams led by domain owners. Project-based → persistent funding. Aligned incentives across business and technology.
5. **Address the AI creator and heavy-user retention crisis proactively.** Track who they are. Stay interviews. Audit flexibility, meaning, community, well-being. **A 51-percent planned-departure rate in your most AI-capable workers is a major risk signal.**
6. **Invest in C-suite AI literacy.** Not data science training — operational understanding of gen AI's potential and limits, enough to ask good questions and set strategic priorities.
7. **Measure transformation success by business-outcome KPIs, not deployment metrics.** Companies deriving majority value focus on deeply transformed domains, not pilot counts. **Define KPIs (e.g., 20-percent incremental value per domain) and hold domain owners accountable.**

## Unresolved Tensions

McKinsey's "people before tech" rhetoric leaves some tensions unresolved:

- **The retention diagnosis may be incomplete.** McKinsey emphasizes flexibility, meaning, community for retaining creators/heavy users. But the underlying assumption is they'll be productive *if* engaged. Possibly 51 percent plan to leave because they see AI disrupting their industry and want to get ahead of it — not because current employers lack meaning. Better culture may not prevent the exodus if the industry is in flux.
- **"People first" vs. "automate first" reality.** McKinsey advocates people-first while presenting gen AI as automating up to 30 percent of business activities by 2030. What does meaning look like for workers in roles being automated? McKinsey speaks to reskilling but doesn't fully address the existential anxiety.
- **The shortcut temptation.** McKinsey's vision (domain transformation, apprenticeship, building leadership bench) is correct but resource-intensive and multi-year. Implementing a few AI tools, laying off workers, declaring victory — McKinsey's research says this is the path to underperformance and talent loss, but doesn't fully explain why so many companies will ignore the counsel.
- **Agentic AI may rapidly invalidate guidance.** McKinsey's 2025–2026 research is published as agentic AI moves from experimental to early deployment. Skills, roles, and domain ownership guidance may need revision as autonomous agents reshape work. McKinsey's actionable guidance is premised on a more human-in-the-loop world than agents are beginning to enable.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Gen AI is a workforce issue, not primarily a tech issue.** 88 percent of gen AI users are in non-technical roles.
2. **Top AI talent is at high flight risk** (51 percent of creators/heavy users plan to leave in 6 months). Compensation is not the lever; flexibility, meaning, community, well-being are.
3. **Shift from roles to skills.** AI-inferred, modular skills mapped to business needs. Quarterly updates.
4. **Domain owners are the critical bottleneck.** 75–150 leaders who bridge business and AI. Only 5 percent of Fortune 500 senior leaders have technical backgrounds.
5. **Building AI muscle in leaders is multi-year and hands-on.** No boot camp suffices. Top learners spend 6–10 hours weekly. Apprenticeship.
6. **Governance ≠ IT.** Chief AI officer coordinates; domain owners execute. Business-led delivery, persistent funding, aligned incentives.
